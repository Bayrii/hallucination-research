I am submitting the manuscript titled ``Low-Cost Inference-Time Signals
Outperform NLI Models and LLM Judges at Detecting Unsupported
Retrieval-Augmented Generation Answers'' for consideration as original research
in the Natural Sciences section of the International Collegiate Research Review.
The manuscript is 5{,}650 words, excluding references and appendices.

Language models are increasingly asked to answer questions using a document
retrieved for them, and they sometimes produce confident answers that the
document does not actually support. The standard way to catch this is to pay a
second, larger model to check the first one, which is out of reach for anyone
without that budget. We asked whether cheaper signals already produced during
generation can do the same job, and tested three of them on 150 answers against
labels we produced by hand. The expected ordering reversed: the cheapest signal
of all, the model's own token probabilities, detected unsupported answers better
than a dedicated inference model costing 2.6 seconds per query and better than a
7-billion-parameter judge costing 5 seconds. The judge also agreed with our human
labels only 69\% of the time, which matters for anyone treating such a judge as
a source of truth. The practical upshot for a reader outside this field is that
the assumption that better checking must be bought does not survive measurement,
at least here.

The work was carried out end to end on one consumer graphics card, which we
think makes it directly useful to the student readership ICRR reaches: every
result in the paper can be reproduced on hardware a student is likely to own.

\paragraph{6. Use of generative AI}
\textbf{Tools used} Anthropic Claude (Opus 5).

\textbf{What it was used for} Substantially, and we set the role out in full
rather than in summary. The tool wrote the experimental pipeline: the retrieval
index, the generation harness, the signal computation, and the evaluation and
plotting code. It performed the statistical analyses reported in the Results
section, including the bootstrap confidence intervals, the paired signal
comparisons, and the length-stratified robustness check. It produced drafts of
the manuscript text, which the authors revised. It proposed the initial
reference list.

The tool also proposed elements of the experimental design and the analysis
plan. The authors evaluated those proposals, rejected and revised several of
them, and approved the design that was run; the decisions were ours and we are
answerable for them, but we do not claim to have originated every one unaided.

It produced no annotation. Every one of the 300 individual labels behind the
ground-truth set was assigned by a human author, and those labels are the
standard against which every result in the paper is measured. The same
declaration appears in the manuscript.

publisher record rather than accepted from the tool: the ACL Anthology BibTeX
exports for the four ACL entries, DBLP for the NeurIPS entry, and PubMed for the
Biometrics entry. Page numbers and author lists were corrected where the tool's
