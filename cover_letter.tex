To the Editors,

\vspace{1em}

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, \textit{``Detecting Hallucinations in
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Can Lightweight Confidence Signals Catch
Unsupported Claims?''}, for consideration.

The paper addresses a practical problem in deploying retrieval-augmented
generation: such systems still produce claims their retrieved context does not
support, and the standard remedy, checking each output with a second, larger
judge model, assumes a compute budget that many teams do not have. We ask how
much of that checking can be recovered from signals already available at
inference time on modest hardware, and we treat computational cost as a reported
outcome rather than an afterthought.

Our central finding runs against the ordering the literature's framing implies.
Across 150 question--context pairs, evaluated against a two-annotator consensus
($\kappa = 0.78$), mean token log-probability, which is free and requires nothing
beyond numbers the generator has already computed, achieved the highest
detection performance (AUROC 0.849). A 184M-parameter NLI cross-encoder reached
0.669 at 2.6 seconds per query, and a 7B LLM-as-judge scored a lower $F_1$ than
the free signal at 5.0 seconds, agreeing with our human annotators only 69.3\%
of the time. We verify that the log-probability result is not reducible to
answer length, and we are explicit about the mechanism we believe underlies it
and about the conditions under which we would expect it to fail.

We think the work suits the venue for three reasons. It is a complete empirical
study with a negative-to-surprising result, conducted end to end on a single
6\,GB consumer GPU, which makes it directly reproducible by readers with similar
constraints. It reports two secondary findings we did not set out to test: that
an instruction-tuned model frequently refuses rather than fabricates when handed
the wrong document, and that an LLM judge systematically violated an explicit
instruction in its own rubric on essentially every applicable case. And it is
candid about its limits: we state plainly where our sample is small, where our
conditions are milder than intended, and where we would expect our headline
result not to transfer.
